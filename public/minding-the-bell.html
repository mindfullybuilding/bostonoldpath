Minding the Bell Is Minding the Sangha

About the Bell

One of Thầy’s precious gifts to the sangha is our tradition of incorporating the bell in our practice. This ritual is unique to our Plum Village tradition. It’s Thầy’s way of inviting us to come back to the present moment and concentrate our awareness at the sound of the bell. It also helps us be aware of each other as a sangha and to practice in harmony. We treat the bell and the inviter with respect as instruments of the Dharma.

When we hear the sound of the bell, we stop what we are doing and come home to our self--- body, mind and breath together ---so we are truly present. Then we know we’re in sangha and not in a meeting. The bell minder’s role is to hold the practice, convey ease and offer their true presence. The way the bell minder picks up the bell, holds the bell and invites the bell already generates a lot of mindfulness and that energy nourishes the sangha. Thầy has said he knows how long someone has been practicing, simply by the way the bell is invited.

Almost any sound can be a “bell of mindfulness.” At the monasteries, at Days of Mindfulness and on retreats, there are many bells of mindfulness besides the meditation hall bell—for instance, the whole sangha will stop and take three breaths at the sound of the telephone or a clock chiming the hour.

At home, a siren, the doorbell, the telephone...anything can be a reminder to stop what we’re doing, take three full breaths and come home to our self. Many friends take three breaths before answering their cell phone. During sangha meetings, the sound of the bell synchronizes our movement so we can “go as a river.” You can find a number of Dharma talks about inviting the bell by Thầy and other monastics.

Bell Practice

Before inviting the bell, we want to be really solid, peaceful and present. When we’re ready to invite the bell, we place it in the palm of our hand and hold it gently. You might like to think of your hand as like a five-petal flower, with the bell in the center. We raise the bell to eye level and breathe in and out three times before we wake it up. We can prepare our self by reciting this gatha, followed by two or three or four in and out breaths. When we are present, calm and centered, we are “qualified” as a bell minder.

Body, speech, and mind in perfect oneness, I send my heart along with the sound of this bell. May the hearers awaken from forgetfulness, and transcend the path of anxiety and sorrow.

You don’t need to remember this gatha—it’s fine to simply say "Hello, dear bell, my friend," or any other words that nourish and center you. Then, we “wake up the bell,” which really means to wake up our self and our friends.

To wake up the bell we gently touch the edge of the bell with the inviter and hold it there, creating a short, muffled sound. This allows everyone to be prepared for a full sound of the bell, so there’s no surprise or startled reaction to the full sound. This is an especially welcome notice at the end of sitting meditation. After waking up the bell, Thầy advises us to take a full in-breath and out-breath before the full sound of the bell.

We invite the bell three times before a meditation, with three full breaths between each sound of the bell. There is no need to hurry. When the Bell Minder enjoys those breaths, it invites the sangha to enjoy them too. It’s good to err on the side of a soft sound rather than a harsh, loud sound if you are unsure of the quality of the bell. Facilitators are encouraged to practice inviting the bell at home and we ought to own a bell to use in our own practice and in order to practice inviting the bell at home. The sangha can order one for you. You might enjoy watching Thầy teach us how to invite the bell:

How Many Bells? When?

Please remember that when you facilitate, the most important factor is to generate mindfulness so the sangha feels your stability. The best way to feel at ease in inviting the bell is to practice many, many times at home. It’s helpful to recite the gatha, too. We wake up the bell each time before inviting a full sound. The bell cadences help create harmony and ease but without your true presence they won’t manifest. Generally, we begin an activity with three bells and end with two very close together.
