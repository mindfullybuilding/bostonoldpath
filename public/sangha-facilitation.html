Facilitating a Sangha Meeting

Minding the Bell is Minding the Sangha

This document intends to offer support so that we don’t need to make the same mistakes as have already been made and re-invent the wheel. It is not meant to create rigid rules and structure instead to invite flexibility and ease.

The facilitator minds the bell and minds the time. We maintain the evening’s schedule, beginning and ending promptly. We also take care to update the Facilitation Log for everyone’s convenience. You will enjoy facilitation and benefit much more if you are familiar with the text and, before picking up the bell, you center yourself in the present.

Each evening, the facilitator welcomes us and guides us through the meeting. Please introduce yourself and welcome any newcomer. (See Introduction to the Plum Village Tradition.) You might explain that we rotate facilitation. Briefly explain the flow of the evening. Remember that being seen and cared for is welcoming and creates ease and safety. Check in and ask if they’ve meditated before and whether they are familiar with our tradition. If appropriate, offer a brief introduction to meditation and our tradition so they may feel comfortable with us. You can share about our practice (see Facilitation in the Plum Village Tradition of Thich Nhat Hanh.)

When beginning any activity (for instance Walking Meditation and Dharma Sharing) be aware they may not understand what we do and offer brief guidance. In particular, it’s helpful to explain our practice of bowing in and bowing out. You can share that we bow before speaking and when we’re done so no one will interrupt. There are several ways to describe the symbolism of bringing our palms together: the hands represent body and mind, together; the hands form a flower bud; or the hands recognize the Buddha nature in the other. Please mention we can also place our hand on our heart or use another signal we are comfortable with to indicate we’d like to share. The Sangha bows back to acknowledge that we are ready to listen deeply. When we are finished we let the Sangha know by bowing or signaling again. Knowing we will not be interrupted creates a safe and harmonious environment.

The best way we can prepare to facilitate is to take care of our own practice on a regular basis so that we can truly embody mindfulness and ease when we facilitate. When others see that we are present, calm and relaxed, it helps them become present, calm and relaxed. Be sure to take time to center yourself-- your peaceful energy is what is transmitted. To support the sangha in this way is a joyful responsibility.

Give clear but brief introductions to each part of the evening. For regular members, we can use only the bells to signal each transition with very few or no words. It’s our tradition that during dharma sharing, the facilitator shares last, if at all.

If you’re a new facilitator, it is a good idea to start by serving as Second Body for a few times and to ask a current facilitator to mentor you. We’re here to support one another no matter how long we may have facilitated.

Before beginning, if you are feeling nervous, take your time before inviting the bell. “Ride your breath.” Remember that when you are mindful, it encourages the whole sangha to come back to their breath.

After facilitating an evening it’s helpful to ask for “Shining Light” and ask for feedback from another facilitator. Newer facilitators can turn to more experienced friends for support but it’s also very helpful for each facilitator to check in with another friend for feedback.

Walking Meditation

Please know it’s possible to offer mindful movement instead of or in addition to walking meditation. The purpose of both is to connect with our body and to be in the present moment. You might remind us we’re not trying to get anywhere, but each step is for the sake of that one step. Our lives are increasingly rushed, challenging and pressured and it feels harder to know how to slow down and rest. When we rush, it creates stress and taking our time like this is a way of being non-violent. We can create peace with each step.

Walking with the sangha is a chance to practice mindfulness of movement and changing visual sensations. Walking meditation allows us to know we are walking. We try to focus on our breath in each moment as we move. We walk just for walking because we’re not going anywhere and there isn’t anything to do. We learn to walk slowly, in a relaxed way, keeping in touch with both our breath and our steps, training our self how to be in the present moment. The facilitator could invite us to practice with gatha such as: With one step, say silently “I have arrived”, and with the next step say, “I am home;” or, "In/ Out" or "Here/Now;" or “ Peace/Joy/Love /Happiness.”

Before standing for walking meditation, you may decide to invite four or three bells. The number isn’t as important as allowing enough space for us to transition from sitting meditation to standing to walking. Give clear guidance before the first bell, with, perhaps, this instruction: “I’ll invite one bell for you to prepare your body to stand; a second bell to stand; a third bell to bow to the sangha and the fourth bell to begin walking.”

Please let the sangha know you’ll invite the bell when it’s time to come back to the circle. We “go as a river, not as droplets” so we remain standing until everyone returns, then invite the bell, bow to the sangha and to sit down.

We try to keep an equal distance between the person in front of us and behind us and adjust pace to maintain it. If it feels right guide the sangha so that everyone begins walking at the same time.

Sitting Meditation

Sitting Meditation begins with three bells spaced with three breaths between each and ends with two bells, close together. Please remember, the purpose of sitting meditation is to enjoy it. We don’t try to attain anything but we simply sit still and see what happens.

You might offer a simple explanation of sitting, posture, connecting with the breath and the importance of stopping, calming, resting and healing. Explain that we sit to calm our minds and bodies, to get clarity and deepen our capacity for mindfulness and concentration so that we can choose where to place our minds during the other 23 hours of our day. It’s a good idea to keep any instruction after Walking Meditation brief so that our minds don’t get busy.

You may offer basic instruction to help the sangha move into meditation. You could invite us to sit comfortably, in an upright position and to attend to our breath. When we are grounded in our breath, we are grounded in the present moment. Sr. Dang Nghiem puts it this way: “sit beautifully.” We can begin to notice changes in our body as we inhale and exhale: notice your abdomen, your chest, your nostrils. You might say “in” “out” as you breathe. We can also observe changes in our mind states.

As facilitator, if we share from our experience, it’s good to be clear that this is our personal experience. We also might invite a bell part way through the meditation and mention that if the mind has wandered, we only need to notice that it has wandered, and then gently and without judgment, bring it back to the breath. Sharing analogies, such as, “thoughts can be like clouds in a windy sky, floating by” can be helpful to the sangha. We are here to practice, to train our mind. You might mention that even though we intend to listen deeply, our mind sometimes wanders and to just bring it back.

Guided Meditation: Most people find that leading a guided meditation skillfully takes a good bit of practice. You can find support for this practice and many meditations in the beginning of Thay’s book, Blooming of a Lotus. If we’re offering a guided meditation that we have not previously done a number of times, we usually practice it, and use it in our personal practice before leading the sangha in it.

Introductions

Introductions are a way to hear everyone’s voice and feel their presence. In order to have time for longer sharing later, it’s important to give clear guidance about brevity. Some facilitators ask us to bow in and “tell us in 7 words or less” and if necessary, remind us if we forget. This is a time to appreciate that we’re practicing in community, with others just like us who are trying to do what we aspire to do. The facilitator may choose a “prompt” that invites everyone to feel included. There’s value in connecting the prompt to the reading you’ve selected. You might say: Please bow in and tell us your name and share----

1. Internal weather report

2. Share something that brought you joy or something that is on your mind.

3. What nourishes you?

4. What challenges you?

5. What brought you contentment today/this week?

6. What do you “do to water wholesome seeds?”

7. If you noticed if your thoughts relate mostly to the future, the past or the present?

8. If you listened deeply to yourself or someone else?

9. What things are easy for you to put effort into? Why?

10. Are there obstacles to growing and deepening your practice?

11. Do you reflect on your conditions of happiness?

12. What “habits” do you have that you rely on to cover up your unhappiness, anxiety, worries and other suffering?

13. What are the conditions for your happiness and joy? ....and countless other ways to invite introductions

Readings at Sangha

We participate in “shared reading” by passing around the book, each person reading a section or a paragraph, if they wish. The first Tuesday, however, is a reading and study of the Five Mindfulness Trainings. Also, in months when there is fifth Tuesday, the facilitator might bring in a different text from Thay that’s not in the current book.

As facilitator, you will find it helpful to ground yourself in the material prior to sangha to allow it to ripen in you. This contact with the reading can help you a plan and guide the flow of the evening For instance, the facilitator might offer a guided meditation relevant to the reading, or share meditation instructions that arise out of the reading. Please remember to mark on the facilitation log where your reading ended/the next reading starts: https://tinyurl.com/mtyzwr6e

Facilitating Dharma Sharing and Guidelines

The facilitator is brief and facilitates with the intention to create a space for others to share safely. We remember that some friends are uncomfortable with sharing and we let everyone know that they can “pass.” And we remember that it’s OK to enjoy the silence especially as this may be the only recent experience of it. We have found that “popcorn” style is a comfortable way to share.

The facilitator shares the Guidelines and ensures we follow them. It’s a good practice to ask at the end if anyone who hasn’t already shared would like to. Rarely, the facilitator will invite the bell to create space when needed, inviting us to come back to our breath.

It’s supportive of the atmosphere when participants take three breaths before speaking, to allow time for the previous person’s speaking to be fully received.

You might explain that Dharma Sharing is an opportunity to practice deep listening and mindful speech and a time to share what we notice in our self. We may speak about what has moved us or how we apply the teachings in daily life. We discover that we share similar difficulties and can learn from others how the practice has helped them. Dharma Sharing is a precious opportunity to benefit from each other’s experience of the practice. Overtime, we may realize that many of us share similar difficulties and aspirations. Sitting, listening and sharing together, we recognize our connection to one another.

Please remember that whatever is shared during the Dharma discussion time is confidential. If a friend shares about a difficulty they are facing, we respect that they may or may not wish to talk about this individually outside of the Dharma discussion time. Each evening the facilitator --

• Shares the guidelines. Sometimes we invite the Sangha to offer the guidelines. For example, sangha members can be invited to share a guideline that is particularly meaningful to them. This helps to reinforce the guidelines in our own minds, as well as show others in the group that guidelines are important to people other than the facilitator.

• Adheres to the guidelines. Facilitation sometimes requires assertiveness. When someone is not following the guidelines, we might need to interject ourselves to remind them. This is a deep practice and invites us to look deeply to find skillful means. Depending on the situation, the person, the difficulty, etc, we need to do our best to respond skillfully. We want to help the person and to protect the Sangha. This might require that we step in during the sharing itself to remind people of the guidelines, or it might be more skillful to wait and speak to the person individually. If someone is disregarding the guidelines and it is not addressed, it can negatively affect the feelings of safety in the group and destroy the credibility of the guidelines.

• Makes good use of the bell. We don’t use the bell as a "reward" or a "reprimand,” but at appropriate intervals as a gentle reminder to come back to our breath and to calm ourselves. We can create a space of several breaths between sharing. If the energy gets high and people jump in without pausing to take in what has just been shared, the facilitator can use the bell skillfully to remind us to come back to our breath. You might say, “Let’s come back to our breath” before you invite it. This is a deep practice.

• Minds the time. You may wish to say “we have about x minutes to share so please gauge your time so everyone has a chance to share.” Then during the sharing, it may be necessary to remind us of the need to be mindful of the time and considerate of the groups desire to create enough space to be able to hear from everyone.

At the end of Dharma Sharing, you may wish to invite the bell and ask for announcements. Then close the evening by inviting the sangha to stand and share the Merit.

Frequently Asked Questions About Facilitating Dharma Sharing

• What should I do if someone talks too long during dharma sharing? You might wait for a few people to share after the long share and give a gentle reminder about the time. Normalize the situation by acknowledging that we all get enthusiastic and lose track of time, but we need to be mindful of how many people we have in our group and that many may have things they would like to share.

• What is “cross talk?

Cross talk is responding to what someone has said. It’s powerful enough to destroy feelings of safety and make another feel that an uncomfortable spot light is shining on them. It’s addressed to one person, thus excluding the rest of the sangha from the exchange.

We all engage in cross talk—in our heads—reacting and wanting to respond to what someone has said. Our practice is to notice when we agree or disagree while someone is speaking. We may be tempted to merely ask a question. But cross talk takes us out of our practice of Deep Listening, whether we want to express sympathy or to express judgement and criticism. But all we’re called upon to do it to listen, to hear what is said and what is unsaid. Nodding our head in agreement or disagreement and our facial expressions indicate our reaction to what is said. Instead, we should “listen like a cow.” Many people have shared that being in sangha is their first experience of being listened to and avoiding cross talk honors that preciousness.

• What should I do if crosstalk happens?

It is important to be assertive. If someone engages in crosstalk, it needs to be addressed right then, doing our best to use skillful means so as not to embarrass the person. We may say “We all have habit energy, we all have the best of intentions and want to help, it is human nature. However, we need to abide by the guidelines so that everyone feels safe.” this statement can be made when offering the Dharma Sharing Guidelines. Then, if it happens, we can refer back to them. If someone is consistently not adhering to guidelines, we might meet with them privately. Healing can happen over tea. We might like to offer flower watering, beneficial regret, i.e., “ I know I might not have been clear when sharing the guidelines”, and gently remind them of the reasons we have guidelines. etc...

• What should I do if someone speaks a second time before everyone who wishes to has had a chance to speak once? Remind the sangha that we make space for everyone to share at least once. Before opening the opportunity for a second share, ask if anyone wants to share a first time. While we don’t single anyone out, we demonstrate that everyone’s sharing is valued.

• What about questions?

Questions are generally not encouraged during Dharma sharing for several reasons. But if someone asks a question it should be addressed to the whole group and if we answer a question we speak to everyone and not just the person who asked.

When someone asks a question, we should let them know not expect an answer right away. The facilitator can assure the group that the question has not been forgotten and that the answer will emerge over time. If questions are encouraged during Dharma sharing time the facilitator must watch that the session does not devolve into a Q&A instead of a heartfelt sharing.

Practice When Someone Beloved of a Sangha Member Dies

Sometimes, a person will share that someone dear to them has died and may request that a bell be invited. At the end of Sangha, maybe after announcements, we invite the sangha to come back to our breath. We invite the person who has experienced the loss to say the person’s name clearly, and the dates of birth and death if they know it. If they have not shared about the person in dharma sharing, we might ask them to share a couple of sentences about that person. We then ask the sangha to come back to their breath, and send the energies of loving-kindness and compassion to the person’s family, loved ones and to their continuation. We invite the bell, and take 3 – 5 breaths, at which point we invite the signal bell and thank the sangha.

It is beautiful to bring the energy of the sangha breathing together in compassion and loving-kindness for the deceased and for the living who are mourning. It feels better to me if a sangha member can share about the deceased during dharma sharing, to help the sangha connect with the energy and have a sense of the meaning of the deceased in the life of our sangha brother or sister. Perhaps saying the full name and date of death and birth also helps to us connect with the person, and to keep this practice special and set aside for people who were very close to, and mourned by a sangha member. As a community, we can certainly look into changing or cultivating this practice in a way that is most useful to us.
